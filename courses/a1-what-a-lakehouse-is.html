What a lakehouse is

Most companies keep their data twice: once as files in a cheap lake, and again, cleaned and loaded, in a warehouse that answers the dashboards. In 2021 four of Databricks' founders wrote a paper arguing that this two-copy design would wither, and named what should replace it. Tonight we read that paper the way a leader should: what problem it says the two copies cause, what a lakehouse actually is once the marketing is removed, and which questions the authors themselves left open.

Part 0

Where we start

Databricks sells a platform; the idea underneath it is older and simpler. Keep one copy of the data as ordinary open files in cloud storage, add a thin layer that records which files make up each version of each table, and let every engine, SQL for the dashboards and Python for the models, read those same files. The six leader sessions work up from that idea to the decision of whether it fits your organisation. The eight analyst sessions build every piece of it by hand on real New York taxi trips, and end on a bench that replays a real table's history in the browser.

Part 1 · covers the three generations

How the data ended up in two places

6 min live

The paper, "Lakehouse: A New Generation of Open Platforms that Unify Data Warehousing and Advanced Analytics", by Michael Armbrust, Ali Ghodsi, Reynold Xin and Matei Zaharia (CIDR 2021), tells the history in three generations. Each one fixed the previous one's biggest problem and created a new one.

LiveGeneration one and two, in the paper's words3 min▶

First generation: operational data was loaded into a central warehouse with the schema decided up front, ready for business intelligence. Two things broke it, the paper says: compute and storage were sold together in an appliance, so you paid for peak load, and more and more data was video, audio and text that a warehouse could not hold at all.

Second generation: land everything as files in a data lake, first on Hadoop and from 2015 on cloud object stores such as S3, ADLS and GCS, and load a subset into a warehouse for the important dashboards. The authors write that this two-tier design is "used at virtually all Fortune 500 enterprises", in their experience. Note who is speaking: the founders of a company that sells the alternative. It is a reasonable description of the industry, and it is also the setup for their argument.

LiveThe four problems the paper names3 min▶

Reliability: keeping two systems consistent takes continuous ETL, and every step can introduce bugs, for example when the lake engine and the warehouse engine disagree on a subtle rule. Staleness: the warehouse lags the lake, often by days. The paper cites a Dimensional Research and Fivetran survey in which 86 percent of analysts use out-of-date data and 62 percent wait on engineering several times a month; that survey was not read for this session, so treat those two numbers as reported. Limited advanced analytics: TensorFlow, PyTorch and XGBoost do not run well on a warehouse, so teams export files, which the authors call a third ETL step. Total cost of ownership: two copies of storage, continuous ETL, and proprietary formats that make leaving expensive.

Real world

The third ETL step. A retailer's demand-forecasting team needs three years of order lines. They live in the warehouse, so a weekly job exports them back out to files in the lake, where the training code can read them. The model now trains on data that went source, lake, warehouse, lake: three hops, three schedules, three places a definition can drift. The paper's point is not that this team did anything wrong. It is that the architecture made the round trip the only option.

Part 2 · covers the definition

Open files, plus a layer that keeps promises

5 min live

Strip the branding and the definition is one sentence long. The paper defines a lakehouse as "a data management system based on low-cost and directly-accessible storage that also provides traditional analytical DBMS management and performance features such as ACID transactions, data versioning, auditing, indexing, caching, and query optimization."

LiveWhat the layer adds, and what it gives up3 min▶

The paper's first key idea is to store data as standard files in an object store and put "a transactional metadata layer on top" that "defines which objects are part of a table version". Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg and Apache Hudi are such layers. Because the layer decides which files are in the table, it can make writes atomic, keep old versions, enforce a schema, hold statistics for skipping files, and check permissions before anyone touches the files.

The authors are explicit about the price: direct access means giving up "some aspects of data independence", the old database promise that the storage format is private and can change freely. In a lakehouse the file format is part of the public interface, because every engine reads it. That is what makes it open, and it is what makes it hard to optimise the way a closed warehouse can.

Self-studyWhere Databricks sits in the definition2 min▶

Databricks is one company's product built on the idea: Delta Lake as the open table format, Unity Catalog as the governance layer (session 3), and managed compute for notebooks, jobs and SQL (sessions 4 and 5). The idea is broader than the product. The general thesis across vendors, and how other platforms answer it, is covered in the lakehouse engine session of the AI infrastructure course; the warehouse side of the story lives in the data warehouse course.

Part 3 · covers what the paper left open

The questions its own authors asked

4 min live

A founding paper that lists its own open questions is worth more than a brochure that lists none. Three of them matter to a leader choosing a platform today, and one of them the analyst bench measures directly.

LiveSpeed is the hard part, and it depends on layout2 min▶

The paper calls SQL performance "perhaps the largest technical question with the Lakehouse approach" and proposes three format-independent fixes: cache hot files on local SSD, keep auxiliary data such as per-file min and max values "which enables data skipping optimizations when the base data is clustered", and choose the data layout. That last clause matters. Statistics only help if similar values sit in the same files. On the analyst bench, 8,000 real taxi trips in 8 files: a JFK query reads all 8 until the files are clustered by pickup zone, then reads 2.

Self-studyThe benchmark, read as a vendor benchmark2 min▶

The paper reports TPC-DS at scale factor 30,000 on clusters of 960 vCPUs: Databricks' Delta Engine ran all 99 queries in 3,302 seconds for $104 on demand, against four unnamed cloud warehouses at 2,996 to 37,283 seconds and $153 to $570. It is a benchmark run by the authors of the system that won it, against competitors they chose not to name, at settings they picked. That does not make it false; it makes it a claim to test on your own workload, which session 6 turns into a decision memo.

Build-along

Map where your data is copied

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No tools and no account. A blank page and your organisation. The point is to see your own architecture in the paper's terms: how many copies, how many hops, and which consumers would read the files directly if they could. Session 6 turns this map into a decision.

List the five data sources your leadership asks about most. Orders, customers, product events, finance ledgers, support tickets: whatever your monthly review depends on. Write each in the left column.

Follow each one to every place it is copied. A lake folder, a warehouse schema, a spreadsheet extract, a feature table for a model. Each copy is a box; each arrow is a job somebody owns.

★ One row, fully labelledorders database -> lake raw/orders nightly, data team, lands +8h -> warehouse mart daily, BI team, lands +1 day -> ML extract (files) weekly, DS team, lands +3 days copies: 3 hops: 3 oldest lag: 3 days

Label every arrow with owner, schedule and lag. The lag is how old the copy is when its consumer reads it. This is the paper's staleness problem measured in your own units.

Circle the consumers that need raw files. Model training, notebooks, anything in Python. In the paper's terms these are the consumers a warehouse serves worst, and they are the reason a lakehouse argument exists at all.

Write the tally and one sentence. "We keep N copies across M hops; the oldest copy is K days stale; the consumers who suffer most are ..." Bring it to session 6.

✗ The map that hides the problemSources -> Data Platform -> Insights (one box in the middle, no owners, no schedules, no lag)

Count the copies before counting the cost. A platform decision made from a vendor diagram compares two pictures. A decision made from your own copy map compares two bills and two waiting times, which is what sessions 5 and 6 need.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which description matches the paper's definition of a lakehouse?

The definition has two halves and both matter: open files any engine can read, and a metadata layer that gives them the guarantees a warehouse used to give. Without the layer it is a lake; without open files it is a warehouse.

2 · The paper says 86 percent of analysts use out-of-date data. How should a leader use that number?

The figure comes from a vendor survey quoted in a vendor-authored paper. It is not worthless and it is not proof. Your own map gives a lag you can defend.

3 · What does a lakehouse give up, according to its own authors?

Openness has a price. A closed warehouse can change its storage format at will to go faster; a lakehouse must keep its files readable by every engine, and win speed back through caching, statistics and layout.
