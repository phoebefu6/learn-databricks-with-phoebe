Lakehouse or warehouse?

Part 0

Where we start

Platform decisions are usually made from someone else's evidence: a vendor's benchmark, a vendor's survey, an analyst's quadrant. None of it is worthless, and none of it is about your data. The habit worth building is to sort every piece of evidence into two piles before deciding, the numbers you measured yourself and the numbers someone with an interest measured for you, and to say out loud why the second pile gets less weight. A lakehouse is the right answer for some organisations and the wrong one for others. Tonight you find out which you are, on your own numbers.

Part 1 · covers the case for the lakehouse

What one copy buys

5 min live

Start with the argument at full strength. The CIDR 2021 paper by Armbrust, Ghodsi, Xin and Zaharia names four problems with the two-tier lake plus warehouse, and each of the lakehouse's design moves is aimed at one of them. If none of the four problems shows up on your copy map, the argument has nothing to fix for you.

LiveThe paper's claim, and who is making it2 min▶

The claim is that the two-tier architecture causes four problems, reliability, staleness, weak support for advanced analytics, and total cost of ownership, and that one copy of open files with a transactional metadata layer on top removes all four at once. The paper defines a lakehouse as "a data management system based on low-cost and directly-accessible storage that also provides traditional analytical DBMS management and performance features such as ACID transactions, data versioning, auditing, indexing, caching, and query optimization."

Now the speaker. Michael Armbrust, Ali Ghodsi, Reynold Xin and Matei Zaharia are Databricks founders and engineers, writing in 2021 about the architecture their company sells. That does not make the argument wrong; it is a well-made argument and the four problems are real. It means the argument is a hypothesis about your organisation, and your copy map from session 1 is the test. If you have one copy and no hop anyone waits on, problem two is already solved for you.

LiveWhat the Delta Lake paper measured about metadata2 min▶

A lake without a metadata layer has to ask the object store which files exist, and listing is slow at scale. The Delta Lake paper (Armbrust et al., PVLDB 2020) reports that a table with 1 million partitions was listed in 108 seconds with Delta, and in 17 seconds with the log cached, while Hive took over an hour at only 10,000 partitions. These are the authors' own measurements of their own system.

What a leader takes from it: the transaction log is not just a reliability feature, it is also how a lakehouse answers "which files are the table?" without walking the whole store. That is the mechanism behind the amber layer in the figure. Whether your tables are anywhere near a million partitions is a question for your team, and the honest answer for most organisations is no.

Self-study"About half of Databricks' workload"2 min▶

The CIDR paper says Delta Lake "is now used in about half of Databricks' workload". That is the authors' claim about their own customers, as of 2021, with no breakdown. It tells you the format was in heavy production use on one vendor's platform at that time. It does not tell you what share of those customers also kept a warehouse, whether the format suited them better than an alternative, or anything about the years since.

Use it the way you would use any adoption claim from a vendor: as a reason to believe the technology is not experimental, and never as a reason to believe it fits you.

Real world

A 500 TB table that mostly did not need to be read. The Delta Lake paper describes a customer with a network-traffic table of about 500 TB, Z-ordered on several fields, where queries skipped 93 percent of the data. It is a customer case as reported by the authors, not an independent study. The lesson for a leader is the mechanism rather than the number: one large copy stays affordable to query only when its files are laid out for the questions people actually ask, and the log's per-file statistics are what let the engine skip the rest.

Part 2 · covers reading the evidence

A vendor benchmark, read honestly

6 min live

Figure 3 of the CIDR paper is the most quoted chart in the lakehouse argument. It is also a textbook example of a vendor benchmark: real, carefully run, and run by the people who built the winner. Here it is with every number the paper gives, and the four questions to ask before anyone in your organisation quotes it.

LiveHow to read it: a claim to test, not a fact to quote3 min▶

The paper reports TPC-DS at scale factor 30,000, each system on 960 vCPUs. Delta Engine finished in 3,302 seconds for $104 on demand, or 3,252 seconds for $56 on spot instances. Four cloud warehouses, not named, took 2,996 to 37,283 seconds and cost $153 to $570.

Read the whole chart before the headline. On duration, one of the unnamed warehouses (2,996 seconds) was faster than Delta Engine; the clear win is on cost, not speed. The benchmark was run by the authors of the winning system, the competitors are unnamed so nobody can check their configuration, the settings were chosen by the side that won, and the data was cached on local SSD first; the paper itself notes Delta Engine was 18 percent slower from a cold cache. None of this makes the result false. It makes it one well-run test on a standard workload, by an interested party, that may or may not resemble your queries. The professional move is to turn it into a test of your own, which is what the build-along does.

LiveThe open questions the authors raised themselves2 min▶

The same paper is unusually candid about what its design does not yet do. Two of its open questions belong in your memo:

The log lives in the object store. Keeping the transaction log as files next to the data is simple and needs no extra service, but the authors note it limits how many transactions per second a table can take. Nightly and hourly loads are fine; thousands of tiny concurrent writes to one table are where an operational database or a warehouse built for that pattern may be the better answer.

One table at a time. Delta, Iceberg and Hudi each commit to a single table per transaction. If your process must change orders and payments together, atomically, that guarantee is not in the 2021 design. Ask any vendor, including Databricks, how they handle it today, and ask to see it work on your case.

Self-studyThe 86 percent staleness figure is a reported survey1 min▶

The paper cites a Dimensional Research and Fivetran survey in which 86 percent of analysts use out-of-date data and 62 percent report waiting on engineering several times a month. The survey itself was not read for this session, so both numbers are reported, cited by an interested party, from a survey commissioned by another vendor in the same market. Your copy map already measured the lag that matters to you. Put your number in the memo, and put the survey in the discounted column with that reason.

Real world

The slide that arrived before the question (constructed). A finance director forwards a vendor deck to the head of data with one line: "They say it is cheaper. Why are we not on it?" The slide shows $104 against $570. The head of data replies with three sentences: which benchmark it is and who ran it; that one competitor was faster on duration in the same chart; and that the team will run its own top twenty dashboard queries on both platforms for two weeks and report cost per run. The reply took ten minutes and turned a forwarded slide into a test with a date on it.

Part 3 · covers the decision

When each one wins

5 min live

The decision turns on who needs to read the data and in what way, much more than on which engine is faster. Three questions get most organisations to an answer, and every answer ends in either a test or a review date.

LiveWhere a warehouse stays the better answer2 min▶

Go back to the four problems in Part 1. A warehouse wins when none of them is biting. The data is tabular and the consumers are dashboards and reports, so there is no ML team exporting files. The team works in SQL and has no plan to change that. The warehouse is already paid for, contracted and operated well, and your copy map shows it is fresh enough for the people who read it. In that organisation a lakehouse migration buys a new platform, a retraining programme and a year of parallel running, in exchange for fixing problems you do not have.

The authors' open questions add two more cases: many small concurrent writes to one table, and changes that must be atomic across several tables. Neither is a reason to rule a lakehouse out, but both are reasons to test before deciding.

LiveWhat to test on your own workload2 min▶

A useful test is small and real. Take one query set your organisation actually runs, for example the twenty queries behind your most-read dashboard, and run it on both options for two weeks with the same data. Measure three things. Cost per run, using the arithmetic from session 5 (DBU rate times DBUs, plus the VMs on classic compute). Freshness, as the lag your copy map measures from source to the reader. Effort, as the hours your team spent keeping both running.

Prices in the memo should carry their basis. The rates used in session 5 are Azure Databricks Premium, East US, pay as you go, read 3 October 2026; AWS and GCP list prices differ, and your contract may differ again.

Self-studyLayout is a design decision on either side3 min▶

Dimensional modelling, star schemas and warehouse internals are not taught here; they live in the data warehouse course. One measured result from the analyst bench belongs in every platform decision, because it holds on either side. On 8,000 real taxi trips in 8 files, clustering the files by pickup zone cut a JFK query from 8 of 8 files read to 2 of 8. The same rewrite made a one-day date query worse, from 2 of 8 files to 8 of 8, because it scattered the time order the daily loads had given. Clustering is a choice of which question gets cheap.

On Databricks, liquid clustering is what Databricks recommends for new tables; the bench runs on delta-rs 1.6.6, which offers Z-order, not CLUSTER BY, so it measures the Z-order version of the same idea. Either way, the decision memo should name the two or three questions that must be cheap, because no layout makes every question cheap.

Real world

Same data volume, opposite right answers (constructed). Two retailers hold about the same amount of data. The first runs a finance and merchandising function on a cloud warehouse; every consumer is a dashboard, the team writes SQL, and the copy map shows one copy, one hop, a lag of a few hours that nobody complains about. Its memo says warehouse, review in a year. The second has a pricing team training models on clickstream and order history; the copy map shows the same orders in three places and a weekly export to files that is three days stale when the model reads it. Its memo says lakehouse, with a two-week test on the pricing queries first. Same volume, different readers, different answers.

Build-along

The decision memo

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No account and no code. You need the copy map from session 1 and the monthly estimate from session 5; if you missed either, write the rows you can and mark the gaps. By the end you have one page your leadership can read in three minutes and your successor can audit in a year.

Paste your copy-map tally from session 1. Copies, hops, the oldest lag, and the consumers who suffer most. This is the context row, and it is the evidence that the four problems are, or are not, present in your organisation.

★ Row 1 · Context (example row, constructed)Copy map, 5 sources: orders: copies 3, hops 3, oldest lag 3 days (ML extract) others: [your rows] Consumers who wait most: demand-forecasting team Problems present: staleness (yes), limited ML support (yes), reliability (one incident this quarter), cost (unknown)

Paste your monthly estimate from session 5. Keep the basis line. A cost without its price basis cannot be compared with anything.

★ Row 1, continued · Cost basisNightly 2-hour job, 5 x DS3 v2: jobs compute $155.40 for 30 nights all-purpose compute $211.65 for 30 nights Serverless SQL warehouse, 2X-Small: $2.80 per hour while running x [your hours per month] Basis: Azure Databricks Premium, East US, pay as you go, read 3 October 2026. AWS and GCP list prices differ. Workload is constructed; your numbers will differ.

Sort the evidence into two columns, and give each discounted item a reason. Trusted means you or your team measured it on your data. Discounted means someone with an interest measured it on theirs. Discounted is not deleted; it stays on the page so everyone can see it was considered.

★ Rows 3 and 4 · EvidenceTRUSTED (measured by us) - copy-map lag: 3 days on the ML extract - estimate: $155.40 per month for the nightly job, Azure list basis - [query times from the two-week test, when it ends] DISCOUNTED (and why) - TPC-DS, $104 vs $153 to $570: run by the platform's authors, competitors unnamed, warm cache; one rival was faster on duration - 86% of analysts on stale data: vendor survey, reported, not read - "about half of Databricks' workload": the authors' own claim, 2021

Define the two-week test. One query set, both options, the same data, three measures. Write what result would change your mind before you run it, or the test becomes a ceremony.

★ Row 5 · Test to runQuery set: the 20 queries behind the weekly revenue dashboard + the demand-forecast training read Run on: current warehouse vs Delta tables on Databricks Duration: two weeks, same source data Measure: cost per run, lag from source to reader, team hours Decides if: one option is cheaper per run AND no staler, with team hours we can staff

Write the decision and the review date. One sentence, one owner, one date, and the result that would reopen it. A decision with no review date quietly becomes permanent.

★ Rows 6 and 7 · Decision and reviewDecision: Run both for now. Move the forecasting data to Delta tables if the two-week test shows lower cost per run and no higher lag. Owner: head of data platform. Review: first week of next quarter. Reopen if: cost per run rises by more than our agreed margin, or the ML extract is still more than a day stale.
✗ The memo that will not survive the next pitchWe chose X because the benchmark says it is 2x faster. (no copy map, no estimate, no named test, no review date, and no word on whose benchmark it was)

Name what you discounted. A decision that names the evidence it discounted, and why, survives the next vendor pitch. When the next deck arrives with a better chart, the memo already says how charts like it are weighed, and the conversation starts from your two-week test instead of from zero.

Homework

Before you send the memo

30 min
  1. Fill every row of the memo with your own numbers, and mark any row still waiting on the two-week test.
  2. Walk the decision tree with one colleague who disagrees with you, and write down where your answers split.
  3. Pick the query set for the test with the team that owns the dashboard, and agree the three measures before anyone runs a query.
  4. Read the CIDR paper's section on open questions and add one line to row 7 about whichever one touches your workload: transactions per second on the log, or changes across several tables.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and leader session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The CIDR paper's TPC-DS chart shows Delta Engine at $104 against $153 to $570 for four unnamed warehouses. How should it enter your decision?

It is a careful benchmark run by the authors of the winner, against unnamed competitors, on a warm cache. That makes it a claim worth testing, not a fact worth quoting. And the same chart shows a warehouse finishing first on duration.

2 · Which limitation did the paper's own authors name as an open question?

The log as files in the store is simple and needs no extra service, at the price of a ceiling on commit rate. The other open question is that Delta, Iceberg and Hudi transact on one table at a time.

3 · Which organisation is most likely better served by staying on its warehouse?

None of the four two-tier problems is biting: no ML reader, no second engine, no staleness complaint, no second copy to pay for. A migration would buy a new platform to fix problems the copy map does not show.
